\begin{abstract}
Surface and basal crevasses determine whether an ice column retains a
connected load-bearing ligament.  The van der Veen models predict whether an
existing crevasse propagates or arrests, but they do not specify a propagation
rate.  This distinction prevents their direct use as a prognostic damage
equation.  Here, the fractured thickness fraction is written as
\(D=D_{\rms}+D_{\rmb}\), and the van der Veen stress-intensity factors are
retained as the fracture calculation.  A single, explicit kinetic assumption
then converts fracture excess into crack-tip speed.  The resulting law
predicts the same arrest depths as the original criterion and declares
full-thickness failure when \(D=1\).  The crack-speed scale is new physics,
not a result of the static criterion, and requires calibration against
time-resolved crevasse growth.
\end{abstract}

\section{Mechanical question}

Crevasse-depth calculations provide a geometric measure of ice damage, but a
static depth is not an evolution law.  In particular, the linear elastic
fracture mechanics (LEFM) models of \citet{vanderveen1998surface} and
\citet{vanderveen1998bottom} calculate the mode-I stress intensity factor at
an assumed crack tip.  They predict propagation when this factor exceeds the
fracture toughness of ice.  They do not calculate
\(\mathrm{D}D/\mathrm{D}t\).

This note makes the smallest additional assumption needed to obtain that rate.
The calculation is separated into two parts.  First, van der Veen mechanics
determines whether each crack tip is above or below its propagation threshold.
Second, an explicitly assumed kinetic function maps the threshold excess to a
crack-tip speed.  A reduced zero-stress version is also given because it leads
to an algebraic right-hand side suitable for a depth-integrated ice model.
The reduced version is a separate approximation and is not the van der Veen
LEFM calculation.

\section{Geometry, boundary conditions, and assumptions}

Consider an ice column of thickness \(H\) measured in meters.  A surface
crevasse penetrates downward a distance \(d_{\rms}\), and a basal crevasse
penetrates upward a distance \(d_{\rmb}\).  The state variables are
\begin{equation}
 D_{\rms}=\frac{d_{\rms}}{H},\qquad
 D_{\rmb}=\frac{d_{\rmb}}{H},\qquad
 D=D_{\rms}+D_{\rmb}.
 \label{eq:damage}
\end{equation}
The model applies while \(0\leq D<1\).  Full-thickness connection occurs at
the first-hitting time
\begin{equation}
 t_{\mathrm{f}}=\inf\{t:D(t)=1\}.
 \label{eq:failure-time}
\end{equation}
At \(t_{\mathrm{f}}\), the column is declared fractured through its thickness;
the damage equation is not continued beyond this event.

The calculation makes five assumptions.
\begin{enumerate}
 \item Surface and basal crevasses are pre-existing mode-I cracks.  The model
       predicts propagation, not nucleation, and does not include healing.
 \item The tensile resistive stress \(R_n\), measured in pascals, is uniform
       with depth.  Ice overburden and water pressure are hydrostatic.
 \item Surface water-column height and basal water pressure are prescribed by
       observations or a separate hydrology model.
 \item Surface and basal cracks do not mechanically interact before
       \(D=1\).  This assumption isolates their separate contributions but
       becomes least accurate when the remaining ligament is short.
 \item The dimensionless fractions are advected with the ice.  Crack-tip speed
       is measured relative to affine vertical deformation of the column.
\end{enumerate}

With horizontal ice velocity \(\boldsymbol{u}\), the material derivative is
\begin{equation}
 \Dt(\,\cdot\,)
 =\frac{\partial(\,\cdot\,)}{\partial t}
 +\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla(\,\cdot\,).
 \label{eq:material-derivative}
\end{equation}
The resistive stress should be calculated normal to the crevasse.  In a
three-dimensional shallow model this gives
\(R_n=2\tau_{nn}+\tau_{tt}\), where \(t\) is parallel to the crevasse;
using \(\tau_{nn}\) alone is not mechanically equivalent
\citep{reynolds2025}.

\section{van der Veen fracture calculation}

The van der Veen criterion is
\begin{equation}
 K_j>\KIc,\qquad j\in\{\rms,\rmb\},
 \label{eq:criterion}
\end{equation}
where \(K_j\) and \(\KIc\) have units of
\(\mathrm{Pa\,m^{1/2}}\).  A crack propagates while
equation~\eqref{eq:criterion} holds and arrests at the first stable depth where
\(K_j=\KIc\).

For a surface crack, let \(a_{\rms}=HD_{\rms}\), let \(y\) increase downward
from the ice surface, and let \(\delta_{\rms}\) be the dry crack length above
the water surface.  The water-column height is
\(h_{\rms}=\pos{a_{\rms}-\delta_{\rms}}\).  The surface stress intensity factor
is
\begin{align}
 K_{\rms}={}&F(\lambda_{\rms})R_n\sqrt{\pi a_{\rms}}
 -\frac{2\rho_{\rmi}g}{\sqrt{\pi a_{\rms}}}
  \int_0^{a_{\rms}}
  yG\left(\lambda_{\rms},\frac{y}{a_{\rms}}\right)\dd y
 \notag\\
 &+\frac{2\rho_{\mathrm{m}}g}{\sqrt{\pi a_{\rms}}}
  \int_0^{a_{\rms}}
  \pos{y-\delta_{\rms}}
  G\left(\lambda_{\rms},\frac{y}{a_{\rms}}\right)\dd y,
 \qquad
 \lambda_{\rms}=\frac{a_{\rms}}{H}.
 \label{eq:surface-sif}
\end{align}
The three terms are opening by resistive stress, closure by ice overburden,
and opening by meltwater pressure.

For a basal crack, let \(a_{\rmb}=HD_{\rmb}\), let \(y\) increase upward from
the bed, let \(p_{\rmb}^{0}\) be water pressure at the crack mouth, and let
\(\rho_{\rmb}\) be the density of water in the crack.  The basal stress
intensity factor is
\begin{equation}
 K_{\rmb}
 =\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi a_{\rmb}}}
  \int_0^{a_{\rmb}}
  \left\{
    R_n-\rho_{\rmi}g(H-y)
    +\pos{p_{\rmb}^{0}-\rho_{\rmb}gy}
  \right\}
  G\left(\lambda_{\rmb},\frac{y}{a_{\rmb}}\right)\dd y,
 \qquad
 \lambda_{\rmb}=\frac{a_{\rmb}}{H}.
 \label{eq:basal-sif}
\end{equation}
Here ice overburden closes the crack, whereas basal water pressure and tensile
resistive stress open it.  Appendix~\ref{sec:weights} gives the finite-slab
weight functions \(F\) and \(G\).

LEFM requires a finite initial flaw because \(K_j\) is not defined at zero
crack length.  The initial condition must therefore specify
\(D_j(0)=D_{j0}>0\).  In addition, the finite-slab weight functions assume a
rectangular body with a single edge crack.  Grounded basal boundary conditions
can change the calculated stress intensity factor, particularly for deep
cracks \citep{jimenez2018}.

\section{Damage evolution}

The static criterion fixes the sign of the crack speed but not its magnitude.
Write the unknown dimensionless kinetic relation as
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal{F}_j(q)=0\quad\text{for }q\leq1,
 \qquad
 \mathcal{F}_j(q)>0\quad\text{for }q>1.
 \label{eq:general-kinetics}
\end{equation}
If \(v_j\) is a characteristic crack speed in
\(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), a local rate law consistent with the
criterion is
\begin{align}
 \Dt D_{\rms}
 &=\frac{v_{\rms}}{H}
   \mathcal{F}_{\rms}\left(\frac{K_{\rms}}{\KIc}\right),
 \label{eq:surface-rate-general}\\
 \Dt D_{\rmb}
 &=\frac{v_{\rmb}}{H}
   \mathcal{F}_{\rmb}\left(\frac{K_{\rmb}}{\KIc}\right).
 \label{eq:basal-rate-general}
\end{align}
These equations have units of inverse time.  They also retain the original
arrest condition because both rates vanish when \(K_j\leq\KIc\).

A minimal closure is a linear positive-part relation,
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal{F}_j(q)=\pos{q-1}.
 \label{eq:linear-kinetics}
\end{equation}
This relation is an assumption.  It is not derived by van der Veen, and the
calculation below should not be interpreted as a measured dynamic fracture
law.  With equation~\eqref{eq:linear-kinetics}, the requested right-hand side
is
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{
 \Dt D
 =\frac{v_{\rms}}{H}
  \pos{\frac{K_{\rms}(D_{\rms})}{\KIc}-1}
 +\frac{v_{\rmb}}{H}
  \pos{\frac{K_{\rmb}(D_{\rmb})}{\KIc}-1},
 \qquad D<1.
 }
 \label{eq:lefm-damage-law}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:lefm-damage-law}, together with the two component
equations, is the prognostic van der Veen damage model.  The calculation
retains the original propagation threshold; the new parameters
\(v_{\rms}\) and \(v_{\rmb}\) set only the transient rate.

\section{Reduced algebraic form}

Some depth-integrated models do not evaluate stress-intensity factors.  A
separate zero-stress approximation places each crack tip where its net opening
stress vanishes \citep{nye1955,nick2010}.  Define
\begin{equation}
 \Sigma=\frac{R_n}{\rho_{\rmi}gH},\qquad
 W_{\rms}=\frac{h_{\rms}}{H},\qquad
 r_{\rms}=\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{m}}}{\rho_{\rmi}},\qquad
 r_{\rmb}=\frac{\rho_{\rmb}}{\rho_{\rmi}},
 \label{eq:dimensionless-groups}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
 A_{\rmb}
 =\frac{H_{\mathrm{ab}}}{H},\qquad
 H_{\mathrm{ab}}
 =H-\frac{p_{\rmb}^{0}}{\rho_{\rmi}g}.
 \label{eq:height-above-buoyancy}
\end{equation}
Here \(\Sigma\) is tensile stress normalized by ice overburden,
\(W_{\rms}\) is fractional surface-water depth, and \(A_{\rmb}\) measures the
ice thickness not supported by basal water pressure.  Assuming water reaches
the basal crack tip, the normalized opening stresses are
\begin{align}
 \Psi_{\rms}
 &=\Sigma+r_{\rms}W_{\rms}-D_{\rms},
 \label{eq:surface-driver}\\
 \Psi_{\rmb}
 &=\Sigma-A_{\rmb}-(r_{\rmb}-1)D_{\rmb}.
 \label{eq:basal-driver}
\end{align}
Positive \(\Psi_j\) opens the corresponding crack.  Applying the same minimal
kinetic assumption gives
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{
 \Dt D
 =\frac{v_{\rms}}{H}
  \pos{\Sigma+r_{\rms}W_{\rms}-D_{\rms}}
 +\frac{v_{\rmb}}{H}
  \pos{\Sigma-A_{\rmb}-(r_{\rmb}-1)D_{\rmb}},
 \qquad D<1.
 }
 \label{eq:reduced-damage-law}
\end{equation}
The two component equations are obtained by retaining the corresponding term
on the right-hand side.

The static limits provide a direct check.  A dry surface crack arrests at
\(D_{\rms}^{\star}=\Sigma\).  A water-filled basal crack arrests at
\begin{equation}
 D_{\rmb}^{\star}
 =\frac{1}{r_{\rmb}-1}\pos{\Sigma-A_{\rmb}},
 \label{eq:basal-arrest}
\end{equation}
provided \(\rho_{\rmb}>\rho_{\rmi}\).  These are the standard zero-stress
crevasse depths.  In contrast, a fully water-filled surface crack has
\(W_{\rms}=D_{\rms}\), and therefore
\begin{equation}
 \Psi_{\rms}=\Sigma+(r_{\rms}-1)D_{\rms}.
 \label{eq:hydrofracture-feedback}
\end{equation}
Freshwater is denser than ice, so \(r_{\rms}>1\).  Once this crack propagates,
equation~\eqref{eq:hydrofracture-feedback} does not admit a positive arrest
depth; the reduced model predicts full-thickness hydrofracture if the water
column remains supplied.

\section{Predictions and limitations}

No observations are analyzed in this note.  The model nevertheless makes
three testable predictions.

First, measured arrest depths should satisfy \(K_j=\KIc\) when the stress,
thickness, and water-pressure boundary conditions are inserted into
equations~\eqref{eq:surface-sif} and \eqref{eq:basal-sif}.  The reduced model
instead predicts the algebraic depths above.  Radar measurements of basal
crevasse height and repeated surface topography could distinguish these
calculations where the required boundary conditions are known.

Second, if \(v_j\) is constant, the assumed linear kinetics predicts
\begin{equation}
 \frac{H\Dt D_j}{\pos{K_j/\KIc-1}}=v_j
 \label{eq:rate-prediction}
\end{equation}
during propagation.  Thus repeated measurements of crevasse depth should
collapse to a constant speed for a given crack family.  A systematic
dependence of the left-hand side on fracture excess would reject the linear
closure while leaving the van der Veen threshold calculation intact.

Third, for \(0<\Sigma<1\), the reduced model predicts that a dry surface crack
arrests at \(D_{\rms}=\Sigma\), whereas a fully water-filled surface crack
propagates through the thickness under the same tensile stress.  This
prediction is conditional on continued water supply.  A hydrology model is
required to determine whether that boundary condition persists.

The most important uncertainty is the kinetic function
\(\mathcal{F}_j\).  Crack interaction, firn structure, spatially varying
fracture toughness, and geometry-dependent weight functions are also omitted.
These omissions affect the transient speed and may shift the arrest depth.
They should be added only when observations require them.

\section{Conclusion}

This note rewrites the van der Veen propagation criterion as an evolution law
for the fractured thickness fraction.  The central result is
equation~\eqref{eq:lefm-damage-law}: surface and basal damage grow only when
their stress-intensity factors exceed ice fracture toughness, and their rates
add until \(D=1\).  The threshold and arrest calculation come from van der
Veen mechanics.  The linear relation between fracture excess and crack speed
is the sole new rate closure.  Time-resolved measurements of crevasse growth
could constrain that relation or replace it without changing the underlying
fracture calculation.

\appendix
\section{Finite-slab weight functions}
\label{sec:weights}

The van der Veen finite-slab functions, as reproduced by
\citet{jimenez2018}, are
\begin{equation}
 F(\lambda)
 =1.12-0.23\lambda+10.55\lambda^2
  -21.72\lambda^3+30.39\lambda^4,
 \label{eq:F}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{align}
 G(\lambda,\gamma)={}&
 \frac{3.52(1-\gamma)}{(1-\lambda)^{3/2}}
 -\frac{4.35-5.28\gamma}{(1-\lambda)^{1/2}}
 \notag\\
 &+\left[
   \frac{1.30-0.30\gamma^{3/2}}{(1-\gamma^2)^{1/2}}
   +0.83-1.76\gamma
 \right]
 \left[1-(1-\gamma)\lambda\right],
 \label{eq:G}
\end{align}
where \(\lambda=a/H\) and \(\gamma=y/a\).  The crack-tip singularity is
integrable.  Numerical quadrature should stop short of \(\lambda=1\), where
the ice column has already met the full-thickness failure condition.
